Rainy Night Drive-Thru
Live concept Case study Case Library

A drive-thru that runs itself in the rain.

A drive-thru restaurant on a wet night, left running on its own. Cars queue round the building through the order board and both windows, customers walk in from the car park under umbrellas, and the asphalt doubles every sign. You do not drive anything: you watch, from nine camera positions, and the scene keeps going.

The brief

Most architectural visualisation shows a building at noon with nobody in it. A drive-thru is the opposite kind of building: it is defined by what moves through it, and it is busiest at night, in weather, when the signs are doing the work.

So the brief was a scene that runs rather than poses — a rainy night with the queue moving, people coming and going and the wet ground doubling every light — in a single page, built from code, and readable in English or Chinese at the press of a button.

Design decisions

The queue is simulated

Each car follows the lane as a spline, keeps its distance from the car ahead and brakes on a curve it could actually stop in. It waits at the order board, the pay window and the pickup window, and gives way to anyone crossing. Brake lights and indicators follow what the car is doing; the wipers never stop.

Rain in one draw call

30,000 streaks are wrapped around the camera in the vertex shader, discarded under the canopies and brightened by the eight nearest light sources, so the rain glows red by the pylon and amber under the street lamps. Splashes, canopy drips and lightning are three more small shaders.

Only code is downloaded

Asphalt, cladding, timber, stone, concrete, grass and glass are painted on canvas at load, each with a normal map derived from its own height field. Signage is drawn the same way, so switching language redraws every sign, menu board and number plate.

How it is built

The lane is one spline. Every stop, painted arrow, lane line and the concrete lane surface is placed by distance along it, so moving a window moves its stop, its arrow and the point where cars brake. The cars are three body profiles extruded across their width, with the glasshouse, pillars, bumpers, lamps and plates added to the profile and merged once per car type.

The ground is a mirror under a layer of asphalt whose opacity is a puddle map: where the map is dark you see the reflection, where it is light you see aggregate and cracks. The same map drives the roughness, so the puddles are also where the highlights are sharpest. The building, the site and the distant city are merged by material at load, so the static scene costs a few dozen draw calls rather than thousands.

Two languages, one switch

The scene opens in English and switches to Chinese from the panel. Every piece of text in the world is a canvas that knows how to redraw itself: the main sign, the pylon, the menu board and the screens inside, the window signs, the clearance bar, the lettering on the lane, the wet-floor sign and the number plates, which change from three letters and three digits to a blue provincial plate. Prices change currency with the language.

What went wrong first

In the first version the dining room was a picture: the glazing carried a painted interior, and customers vanished the moment they reached the door. Building the room for real — tables, pendant lamps, kiosks, people eating — meant customers now had to get through the glass, so the door bay became its own pair of sliding leaves that open when anyone comes within a couple of metres.

The headlight beams were open cones with a flat fade along their length. Seen from the side, the walls of the cone showed as a pale slab with a hard edge. They now fade towards their silhouette as well as along their length, so a beam reads as light in rain rather than as a sheet of glass, and the street-lamp cones use the same shader.

Outcome

A scene that holds attention without a script: six to eight cars working through the lane, customers walking in and out, nine camera presets and a live count of the queue, in a single page with nothing to download beyond the code. It runs in a current desktop browser; on a phone you can orbit and switch cameras.
